# IBM's Pillars of Trust — An Industry Perspective on Responsible AI

## Why look at an industry perspective?

AI governance does not exist in a single form.

Regulators, governments, technology companies and enterprises approach responsible AI from different contexts and with different priorities. A regulatory framework may focus on sector-specific risks and obligations, while an enterprise technology perspective may focus more directly on the characteristics that make an AI system trustworthy.

IBM provides one such industry perspective through its **Pillars of Trust**. IBM describes trustworthy AI through five core characteristics and uses these principles across its research, technology and enterprise AI work.

This perspective is useful because it moves the discussion beyond *whether an organisation has an AI policy* and towards a more fundamental question:

> **What characteristics should an AI system demonstrate if people are expected to trust it?**

---

## A different lens on AI governance

The distinction between **trustworthy AI** and **AI governance** is important.

The pillars describe qualities that should be present in an AI system and its use. Governance provides the organisational mechanisms needed to establish, assess, monitor and maintain those qualities.

In other words:

**Trustworthiness describes the outcome we want to achieve.**

**Governance provides the structures and processes that help us achieve and sustain it.**

IBM's more recent guidance on implementing AI governance illustrates this operational layer through activities such as establishing purpose and scope, defining roles and authority, creating policies and standards, maintaining an AI inventory, assessing risk, integrating controls into development workflows, and continuously monitoring AI in production.

This distinction matters because a trustworthy AI principle is not useful simply because it appears in a policy document. It has to influence how an organisation designs, deploys, operates and reviews AI.

---

## What the industry perspective adds

Looking at IBM's approach highlights several ideas that are easy to lose when AI governance is treated primarily as a compliance exercise.

### 1. Trust is a system property

Trust cannot be created by a single control.

An AI system may be technically accurate and still raise questions about fairness, privacy or explainability. Conversely, extensive documentation does not compensate for a system that performs unreliably.

The value of the pillars is therefore not in considering them independently, but in recognising that trustworthiness is multidimensional.

IBM's research describes these dimensions as characteristics that need to be assessed and, where necessary, mitigated rather than simply assumed to exist.

### 2. Context matters

There is no universal definition of what “trust” looks like in practice.

IBM explicitly notes that organisations use AI differently and that trust can look different across industries and use cases.

That has an important governance implication:

**The principles may be broadly applicable, but their implementation cannot be entirely generic.**

The level of explanation required, the acceptable degree of risk, the sensitivity of the data, the consequences of an error and the people who need to exercise oversight will vary according to context.

### 3. Governance has to operate throughout the lifecycle

Responsible AI cannot be treated as a review performed immediately before deployment.

IBM's governance guidance places emphasis on visibility, risk assessment, controls, approvals and monitoring across the AI lifecycle.

This changes the governance question from:

> *“Is this AI system compliant?”*

to something closer to:

> *“How do we know this system continues to behave as intended as it is developed, deployed, changed and used?”*

That is a much more demanding — and much more useful — question.

---

## From principles to organisational capability

The industry perspective also reinforces something I have encountered repeatedly while exploring AI adoption:

**Responsible AI is ultimately an organisational capability.**

It requires more than technical controls.

People need to know when AI may be used, when human judgement is required, what risks to look for, how to challenge an output and what to do when something goes wrong.

That means governance connects naturally with:

- leadership and accountability
- risk management
- technology and data practices
- policies and standards
- employee capability
- AI literacy
- monitoring and assurance
- change and adoption

IBM's own guidance emphasises human oversight and accountability alongside technical governance mechanisms, reinforcing the idea that responsible AI cannot be reduced to model-level controls alone.

---

## My takeaway

The value of an industry perspective is not in finding *another framework to follow*.

It is in learning to recognise the underlying questions that recur across different approaches to responsible AI:

**Can the organisation understand what the AI is doing?**

**Can it identify and manage unacceptable risk?**

**Can it demonstrate that the system continues to behave as intended?**

**Does someone remain accountable for the outcome?**

**Can people use the technology responsibly in the context in which it operates?**

These questions are broader than any single industry or regulation.

That is why I see IBM's Pillars of Trust as a useful **industry lens on responsible AI** — not as a replacement for sector-specific governance, but as another way of understanding what trustworthy AI needs to look like when it moves from principle into enterprise use.

---

## From understanding to practice

The final step is not another framework.

It is application.

A governance principle becomes meaningful when it changes a decision, a process or a behaviour.

That is the thinking behind the **Responsible AI Decision Simulator** in the Practical Application section of this repository: taking responsible AI out of the abstract and placing it where it ultimately matters — in the decisions people make when using AI at work.

**Principle → Risk → Decision → Behaviour → Impact**
